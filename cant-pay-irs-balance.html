What to do when you owe the IRS and can't pay it all

Last updated 2026-11-02 · Situations and problems
Short answer

If you owe the IRS and cannot pay in full, file on time anyway, pay what you can, and contact the IRS about a payment plan, a pause in collection if you cannot cover basic living costs, or in some cases an offer in compromise. Penalties and interest keep running until the balance is paid.

Where you stand

Opening a bill you cannot cover is stressful, but it is a common situation, and the IRS has several routes for people who owe more than they can pay at once. None of them requires you to have the full amount in hand.

The first fact to hold onto is that an unpaid balance does not stay still. Interest builds on unpaid tax, penalties and earlier interest until the balance is paid in full. The late-payment penalty is generally 0.5% of the unpaid tax for each month or part of a month, up to a cap of 25% of the unpaid tax. The IRS page describes it as lower during an approved payment plan and higher after a notice of intent to levy. Interest rates are reset on a schedule, so check the IRS interest page for the current figure rather than relying on a number you read elsewhere.

That is why the practical goal is usually to get some arrangement in place soon, even a modest one.

Options compared

These are the main paths the IRS describes. Which one fits depends on your income, your expenses, what you own and whether your returns are filed. The IRS decides each case, so treat this as a map, not a prediction.

  • Pay what you can now. A partial payment lowers the balance that interest and penalties build on, and you can still arrange a plan for the rest.
  • A short-term or long-term payment plan. Many individuals can apply online when they owe under the IRS threshold and have filed all returns. If you cannot apply online, you can request a monthly plan by phone or by mailing Form 9465. Setup fees vary by plan type and how you pay, so check the IRS page for current amounts.
  • Currently not collectible status. If paying would leave you unable to meet basic living expenses, you can ask the IRS to delay collection. The debt is not cancelled, interest and penalties keep accruing, a lien notice may still be filed, and refunds may be applied to the balance. The IRS may ask for financial statements and proof.
  • An offer in compromise. This lets some people settle for less than the full amount, but the IRS generally will not accept an offer if you can pay in full another way. It looks at income, expenses and equity in assets, and it requires that required returns are filed and required estimated payments are made. An application fee applies unless you qualify as low income, so check the IRS page. The IRS Pre-Qualifier tool is the starting point.

A quick example of how people sort this out

Suppose a freelance designer files her return on time and finds she owes more than she saved. She cannot pay it all, but she can send a portion now and about the same amount each month. For her, a monthly payment plan is the natural fit, and she would want to confirm that her returns for every other year are filed, since the online plan requires that.

Now suppose a retiree whose only income barely covers rent and medicine receives a bill. A monthly payment would mean skipping essentials. That is the situation the currently-not-collectible option is built around, and he would expect to be asked for detailed financial information.

What not to do

Do not skip filing because you cannot pay. Filing and paying are separate obligations, and the late-filing penalty is a different and larger charge than the late-payment penalty. File by the due date even if the check will have to come later.

Do not ignore the notices. A payment plan or a request for a pause is much easier to arrange before the IRS escalates. The IRS page notes that enforced collection is generally not taken while a plan is being considered or in effect, for 30 days after a rejection or termination, or during an appeal.

Do not agree to a plan you cannot keep. Defaulting can bring a reinstatement fee, continued penalties and interest, your refunds applied to the debt, and possible lien or levy action. A smaller payment you can actually make is better than an ambitious one you miss.

If you disagree or a request is denied

You have appeal rights. If the IRS rejects or ends a payment plan, the Collection Appeals Program is available, and your letter explains the window, which is generally 30 days for those decisions. If the IRS has sent a final notice of intent to levy, you may also have a right to a Collection Due Process hearing, and the deadline is printed on that notice. Read the notice itself for the exact date.

Frequently asked questions

Should I still file my return if I can't pay?

Yes. File by the due date even if you cannot pay in full. Filing late adds its own penalty, and an extension to file does not extend the time to pay.

Will the IRS stop charging interest if I set up a payment plan?

No. The IRS says penalties and interest continue until the balance is paid in full, although the late-payment penalty rate can be lower while an approved plan is in effect.

What does currently not collectible mean?

It means the IRS agrees to delay collection because paying would keep you from meeting basic living expenses. The debt remains, interest and penalties continue, and the IRS reviews your finances periodically.

Is an offer in compromise a realistic option for me?

It depends on your income, expenses and asset equity. The IRS generally will not accept an offer if you can pay in full another way. Its Pre-Qualifier tool is the usual first step.

Do I have to use the IRS website for a payment plan?

No. If you cannot apply online, you can request a monthly plan by phone or by mailing Form 9465, though fees for those routes are generally higher than online.
