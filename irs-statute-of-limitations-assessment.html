How Long Does the IRS Have to Assess Additional Tax?

Last updated 2026-11-02 · Guides and rights
Short answer

The IRS generally has three years from the date you file to assess additional tax. The period can be six years if more than 25% of gross income was left off, and there is no limit if no return was filed or the return was fraudulent. A written agreement can extend it.

The short version

Tax law puts a time limit on how long the IRS can add to the tax on a return. This limit is called the assessment statute of limitations, and it is found in section 6501 of the Internal Revenue Code. Assessment means the IRS formally records additional tax as owed. Once that period ends, the IRS generally cannot assess more for that return.

The details matter, because the three-year rule is only the starting point, and several situations change the clock. If you are holding an old notice and wondering whether it is too late for the IRS to act, this page will help you ask the right questions, but it cannot tell you the answer for your particular return.

The general rule: three years from filing

In the ordinary case, the IRS has three years after the return was filed to assess tax. The clock is tied to when the return was filed, so the filing date is one of the most useful facts to confirm. A transcript from your IRS Online Account can help you check what the IRS shows as filed.

Take a worker who filed a return for a given year and receives a notice about a mismatched form some time later. The first thing to establish is when that return was filed, since that anchors the period. Whether the notice was sent inside the period is a separate question to settle with the facts in hand.

When the period is longer

Two common situations stretch the standard period.

  • Six years applies when the gross income left off a return is more than 25% of the gross income that was stated on it.
  • A six-year rule also applies to certain omitted income from foreign financial assets above a threshold set in the statute. Check the statute and IRS guidance for the current rules.
  • Written agreement: you and the IRS can agree in writing, before the period expires, to extend it. The IRS must tell you that you may refuse the extension or limit it.
  • The statute contains other exceptions that are not covered here. If your situation is unusual, the full text is worth reading or discussing with a professional.

When there is no time limit

There is no time limit on assessment when no return was filed. There is also none when a return was false or fraudulent and filed with intent to evade tax. This is why a notice about an old year without a filed return is a different situation from a notice about a return you filed on time.

That does not mean every old matter stays open forever. It means the answer to how long depends on whether a return was filed and what it contained.

What the time limit does not tell you

Knowing the assessment period does not answer every question about an old notice. Other time limits exist for different purposes, such as how long you have to claim a refund, and they run on different clocks. The general refund rule is the later of three years from filing or two years from payment.

Also, a date printed on your notice always controls what you must do now. If you believe the IRS is too late, say so in your written response, state the filing date you rely on, and attach support. You still need to answer by the date on the notice. IRS Audit Defense Pro is software that helps you organize those facts and draft a letter you review and send yourself; it does not decide whether the period has run.

Why record retention follows the same clock

The IRS advises keeping records for as long as they may become material, which generally means until the limitations period for the return expires. Its audits guidance says to keep return records at least three years from filing, and longer in some cases. The IRS recordkeeping page adds that there is no fixed schedule: keep documents as long as you might need them to prove income or deductions.

In practice, many people keep tax returns and supporting records longer than the minimum, especially for years with unfiled or amended returns, or where income was complicated. Keeping a simple index of what you hold, by year, makes any future response quicker.

Frequently asked questions

Does the three years start when I file or when the return is due?

The general rule in the statute runs from the date the return was filed. Confirm your filing date with your records or an IRS transcript, and check the statute or a professional if timing is close.

What if I never filed a return for a year?

There is no time limit on assessment when no return was filed. Filing past-due returns is usually worth looking into, and the IRS has guidance on how to do it.

Can the IRS ask me to extend the period?

It can ask for a written agreement made before the period ends. The IRS must tell you that you may refuse or limit the extension.

Is the statute of limitations on assessment the same as on collection?

No. This page covers only the time limit to assess additional tax. Collection and refund claims follow separate rules, so check the IRS pages that cover those topics.

How long should I keep my tax records?

The IRS guidance is generally until the limitations period for the return expires, and at least three years from filing. Longer is common if your situation is complicated.
