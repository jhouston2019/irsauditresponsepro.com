Unreported income notice: how a mismatch happens and how to reply

Last updated 2026-11-02 · Types of audits and examinations
Short answer

A notice about unreported income usually means a payer sent the IRS a form, such as a 1099-NEC or 1099-K, that does not match your return. The notice is not a bill by itself. Compare each form to your records, then agree, partly agree or disagree in writing by the date on the notice.

Why these notices arrive

The IRS compares your return with forms that employers, clients, banks and platforms send in. When a form shows income your return does not, the IRS may propose a change. A CP2000 is the best-known version. The IRS says it proposes changes because third-party information on file does not match the return, that it is not a bill, and that the change may increase tax, decrease it or leave it unchanged. It does not call it an audit.

How gig and contractor forms cause mismatches

If a client paid you as a contractor, expect a Form 1099-NEC. The IRS says that form reports payments to nonemployees for services in a trade or business, while employee wages go on Form W-2. The IRS also says gig income must be reported even if it was part-time or temporary, and even if it was not reported on a 1099-K or 1099-NEC or was paid in cash, property or virtual currency.

So mismatches come from several directions: a form you never received, one that arrived after you filed, two forms covering the same payment, or income you forgot. Each has a different fix.

Example: a designer with three clients

Picture a designer who filed using her bank deposits and later gets a notice. One client sent a 1099-NEC that she forgot to count; a second client's form double-counted an invoice that was also paid through a payment app; the third payment was a refund of her own expense. Her response would agree with the first item, explain the second with invoices and statements, and show the third with the original receipt.

Notice what makes that work: each form is matched to a document, one at a time.

Read these parts of your notice first

Before you gather anything, find the facts that control your next steps.

  • The response date printed on the notice
  • Which forms and amounts the IRS says are missing
  • The explanation of any proposed change
  • How to respond (mail, fax or online) and where to send documents

Your options

Most people end up in one of three places. If the IRS is right, you agree and follow the instructions for paying or arranging payment. If the IRS is partly right, you agree with some items and document the rest. If the IRS is wrong, for example because the income is not yours or was already included, you disagree and explain with records.

For a CP2000 specifically, read the IRS notice page and the CP2000 pages on this site. The response date is the one on your notice; do not rely on a general number.

Tax effects to keep in mind

Added business income can also change self-employment tax. The IRS describes it as Social Security and Medicare tax, figured on Schedule SE, with rates and a wage base that change each year; check the IRS page for current figures. A corrected return or response may therefore change more than one line.

Frequently asked questions

Is an unreported income notice an audit?

Not necessarily. The IRS does not call a CP2000 an audit. It proposes changes after a mismatch with third-party information.

What if I never got the 1099?

The IRS says gig income is reportable even if you never received a form for it. Check your own records and the payer.

What if the form is wrong?

Explain why with documents, such as invoices or statements, and consider asking the payer for a corrected form.

Does a CP2000 mean I owe money?

The IRS says it is not a bill. The proposed change may increase tax, decrease it or leave it unchanged.

How do I find the deadline?

Look at the response date on your notice. General pages, including this one, cannot give your date.
