Home office deduction audit: proving the space qualifies

Last updated 2026-11-02 · Types of audits and examinations
Short answer

In a home office exam the IRS wants to see that part of your home is used regularly and exclusively for business, and how you figured the deduction. Gather floor measurements, photos, and bills for the year, and be ready to explain which calculation method you used.

Where you stand

A letter asking about your home office usually names the deduction line and asks you to substantiate it. That is a request for documents, not a conclusion. Start by deciding which calculation method you used, because the records you need depend on it.

Two methods, one basic test

The IRS describes a simplified option that multiplies a set rate by the square footage of the home used for business, with a cap on the number of square feet. The rate and cap are time-sensitive, so check the IRS page for the current figures. The regular method, covered in IRS Publication 587, calculates actual expenses. The IRS page for the simplified method says both methods require that part of the home be used exclusively and regularly for business, and that the simplified method changes how the deduction is calculated, not who qualifies.

  • Exclusive use: the space is used only for business
  • Regular use: the space is used on an ongoing basis, not occasionally
  • Simplified method: square footage drives the calculation
  • Regular method: actual household expenses, allocated to the business space

Example: remote bookkeeper in a spare bedroom

Consider a bookkeeper who works for several clients from a spare bedroom. The room holds a desk, shelving for client files and a printer, and nothing else. Useful records would include a simple floor plan with measurements, dated photos, a client list showing work performed there, and the lease or mortgage statement showing the home's total size.

Contrast this with a guest room where a desk sits beside a daybed. The examiner is likely to ask how the room is used when no work is happening. Be ready to answer honestly, because the exclusivity requirement is the point most often tested.

Records to gather

If you used the simplified method, you mainly need to prove the square footage and the business use. If you used the regular method, you also need the household bills for the year and a clear way you split them.

  • Measurements of the business space and the whole home
  • Photos of the space as it is used
  • Utility, insurance, and mortgage interest or rent records, if you used the regular method
  • Evidence that your business relies on the space, such as client records or a lease clause

What not to do

Do not claim more square footage than you measured. Do not describe the space as exclusive if family members or guests use it. Do not guess at expenses; use statements. If you realize you made a mistake, say so in your response and ask a qualified practitioner how to correct it.

Who can help

Many people answer a home office question on their own, because the records are few. If your situation involves a business owner, shared spaces, or multiple years, you may want an attorney, CPA or enrolled agent to review before you send.

Frequently asked questions

Does the simplified method skip the exclusive-use test?

No. The IRS says both methods require that part of the home be used exclusively and regularly for business.

What is the simplified rate?

The IRS publishes a rate per square foot and a maximum number of square feet. Check the IRS page for the current figures.

Can I deduct a room I also use for family?

The IRS requires exclusive use of the space for business. If the room serves two purposes, discuss with a qualified practitioner whether the deduction applies.

What is Publication 587?

It is the IRS publication on business use of your home and covers the regular method. Read the current edition.

What proof is simplest to provide?

A measured floor plan, dated photos and a list showing the business activity done in the space.
