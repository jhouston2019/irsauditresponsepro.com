Part of your paycheck is going to the IRS: what that means

Last updated 2026-11-02 · Situations and problems
Short answer

People often say wage garnishment for what the IRS calls a wage levy. It keeps taking part of your pay until released. Some pay is protected based on filing status and dependents. Contact the IRS using the number on the levy about release, hardship or a payment plan.

Garnishment, levy: same thing?

If your payroll department told you the IRS is garnishing your wages, you are hearing the everyday word for something the IRS calls a wage levy. IRS materials use the word levy, and they use seize rather than garnish. This page uses both terms so you can find it, but when you talk to the IRS, say levy.

A wage levy is a legal demand sent to your employer to turn over part of your pay toward a tax debt. It is one kind of levy, alongside bank levies, and it comes after the IRS has gone through its notice steps. The IRS describes a levy as a seizure of property to satisfy a tax debt, while a lien is a claim against property that does not itself take anything.

How the mechanics work

A wage levy is continuous. According to the IRS internal guidance, it reaches wages you earn in the future until the levy is released, so a single pay period is not the end of it. This is the main difference from a one-time bank levy.

Part of your wages is exempt from the levy. How much depends on your filing status and the number of dependents you claim. The levy paperwork includes a Statement of Dependents and Filing Status that your employer gives you, and the IRS guidance says you must return it quickly. If you do not, the exempt amount is calculated as if you were married filing separately with no dependents, which usually protects less of your pay. Take that form seriously and ask your payroll department for it on the first day you hear about the levy.

Ways it can end

Federal law requires the IRS to release a levy in specific situations. These include payment in full, a liability that can no longer be enforced because time has run out, release that will help collect the tax, an installment agreement that covers the debt, and a levy that causes economic hardship.

For wage levies, the IRS page says that where the levy is causing immediate hardship, it must be released. To ask, call the number on the levy or related correspondence with your financial details ready. A release does not cancel the debt, and the IRS can ask for a payment arrangement. If a hardship request is denied, you can appeal under the Collection Appeals Program explained in Publication 1660.

Two scenarios

Consider a warehouse supervisor with two children whose paycheck is cut sharply in one pay period. She gets the dependents form back to payroll right away, then calls the IRS to explain her household costs and asks for a payment plan. If a plan is approved, the law points toward release of a levy that the plan does not allow.

Now consider a driver who changed jobs while the levy was still in place. He tells the IRS his current situation and asks how a release or a payment arrangement would work. In both cases, the first move is a phone call, not a stack of paper.

Your hearing rights

Before most levies, the IRS must send a written notice of your right to a hearing at least 30 days before the first levy for that tax period. A timely hearing request goes to the IRS Independent Office of Appeals, which considers collection alternatives. After the hearing determination, you generally have 30 days to petition Tax Court. If the date for requesting a hearing has passed, a late request may be considered an equivalent hearing, without court review. Your notice shows the exact dates.

Frequently asked questions

Is an IRS wage levy the same as a court-ordered garnishment?

People use the terms loosely. IRS materials use the word levy, and this page makes no claim about an official legal distinction between the two. Use the term levy when you contact the IRS.

Will the levy stop after one paycheck?

No. A wage levy is continuous and reaches future wages until the IRS releases it.

How is the protected part of my pay figured?

It is based on your filing status and dependents, using the information you give on the Statement of Dependents and Filing Status. Return it promptly or a default calculation is used.

Does a release cancel the debt?

No. A release stops the levy. The tax debt, along with penalties and interest, remains until it is paid or otherwise resolved.

Who do I call to ask for a release?

Use the number on the levy or on your related IRS correspondence, and have your financial information ready when you call.
