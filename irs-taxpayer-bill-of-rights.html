The IRS Taxpayer Bill of Rights, in Plain English

Last updated 2026-11-02 · Guides and rights
Short answer

The Taxpayer Bill of Rights lists ten rights, including the right to be informed, to challenge the IRS's position and be heard, to appeal to an independent forum, and to retain representation. They are summarized in IRS Publication 1, and federal law directs the IRS to honor them.

The ten rights at a glance

IRS Publication 1 lists ten rights, and the same ten appear in the tax code. Think of them as a checklist you can hold up against any letter you receive. Here is each one in everyday language.

  • The right to be informed: you are entitled to understand what the IRS is asking of you and why, including in notices and letters.
  • The right to quality service: you can expect the IRS to serve you well when you contact it.
  • The right to pay no more than the correct amount of tax: the goal is the right number, not the biggest one.
  • The right to challenge the IRS's position and be heard: you can disagree, explain why, and send support.
  • The right to appeal in an independent forum: a disagreement can be reviewed by someone outside the office that made the decision.
  • The right to finality: matters should come to an end, which is why time limits such as the one on assessing tax exist.
  • The right to privacy: the way the IRS looks into your affairs should respect your privacy.
  • The right to confidentiality: the information you give the IRS should be kept confidential.
  • The right to retain representation: you may choose someone to act for you, or represent yourself.
  • The right to a fair and just tax system: the rules should be applied fairly, with your circumstances in mind.

Where these rights come from

The list is not just a poster. A paragraph of the Internal Revenue Code, section 7803(a)(3), directs the IRS Commissioner to make sure employees are familiar with these ten rights and act consistently with them. It was added by Congress in late 2015.

It helps to know what that paragraph does and does not do. The rights themselves are granted by other parts of the Code and by IRS procedures, such as the rules on interviews and appeals. The Bill of Rights collects them in one place and tells the IRS to honor them, so it works best as a map to the specific rules behind each right.

How each right can show up in your mail

Most people meet these rights as small practical details in a notice or audit letter rather than as headlines. A few examples:

  • Informed: the notice number, the response date, and the explanation of proposed changes are there so you can see what is being asked. If something is unclear, you can ask for it to be explained.
  • Challenge and be heard: a notice that proposes a change normally explains how to disagree and what to include, such as a signed statement and supporting documents.
  • Appeal: many audit and collection letters describe how to ask for a review by the IRS Independent Office of Appeals, which is a separate part of the IRS. Asking does not give up your right to go to court later.
  • Representation: if you clearly say you want to consult an attorney, CPA, enrolled agent or other authorized representative during an interview, the IRS generally must pause it, with some exceptions. A representative with a written power of attorney can attend in your place.
  • Privacy and confidentiality: the IRS generally must give reasonable advance notice before contacting third parties about your case, and you can ask who was contacted.
  • Recording: you can ask in advance to audio-record an in-person interview using your own equipment.

Using your rights when you disagree

Suppose a retired schoolteacher gets a letter proposing a change to a pension distribution and believes the form was reported wrongly. The right to challenge and be heard means she can answer in writing, explain the mismatch, and attach the corrected statement she has. The right to appeal means that if the examiner still disagrees, the letter usually points to a next step rather than a dead end.

In practice, the strongest use of these rights is boring and procedural: respond by the date on the notice, keep copies of everything, and state clearly which items you agree with and which you do not. What the Bill of Rights does not do

Having rights does not mean a notice goes away or that a deadline is optional. Deadlines printed on a notice still apply, and some, like the Tax Court filing window after a notice of deficiency, cannot be extended. The rights also do not decide who is correct about a number. They protect your ability to be heard and to have the matter reviewed.

Nor do they replace professional judgment. If your situation involves a large dispute, a court filing, or collection action, you may want to read about who can represent you and how the Taxpayer Advocate Service can help when something has stalled. Frequently asked questions

Is the Taxpayer Bill of Rights a law?

The rights are summarized in IRS Publication 1, and a paragraph of the Internal Revenue Code, section 7803(a)(3), directs the IRS to ensure its employees act consistently with them. The underlying protections come from other parts of the Code and IRS procedures.

Can I record my IRS audit interview?

For an in-person interview, you can ask in advance to make an audio recording with your own equipment, and you pay for it. The IRS publication on examinations explains how to make the request, so check the current version for the steps.

Do I have to hire someone to use these rights?

No. The right to retain representation includes the choice to represent yourself. If you want someone else to speak for you, that person must be authorized to practice before the IRS and you generally sign a power of attorney.

Where can I get help if the IRS is not honoring a right or has not answered?

The Taxpayer Advocate Service is an independent office within the IRS that may help when a problem causes financial hardship or has gone unresolved. Eligibility and how to ask are on the Taxpayer Advocate Service website.

Does asking for an appeal waive my right to go to court?

According to the IRS, using the Independent Office of Appeals is optional and does not waive your right to go to court. Court deadlines, however, still run, so check the dates printed on your notice.
