CP90 and CP297: what to do with a final notice of intent to levy

Last updated 2026-11-02 · IRS notices and letters
Short answer

A CP90 or CP297 is a final notice that the IRS intends to levy, and it comes with a right to request a Collection Due Process hearing using Form 12153. The request window is short and printed on your notice, so read it first and decide what to do before that date passes.

What these two notices are

CP90 and CP297 are two versions of the same kind of letter. Each tells you that the IRS intends to levy, which means to take property or money to cover a tax balance that is still unpaid. Each also tells you that you have a right to ask for a Collection Due Process hearing, often shortened to CDP, before that happens.

The difference between the two numbers is not something the IRS pages explain in detail, so the practical move is to treat them the same way: find the notice number in the corner of your letter, read the instructions on it, and follow those rather than anything generic you read online.

Why the date on the notice matters more than anything else

A hearing request is time-limited. The IRS pages for these two notices do not print a number of days, and they point you to the notice itself. A separate IRS list of letters that offer an appeal shows CP90 and CP297 with a Form 12153 request due within 30 days from the date of the letter. Because different IRS pages count from different starting points (the date of the letter in one place, receipt in another), the safest reading is the deadline printed on your notice, which is generally about 30 days.

If you are reading this with the notice already sitting in a pile, check the date today. A request that arrives late may still get a more limited kind of hearing, but the full set of hearing rights is tied to the on-time request.

Read these parts of your notice first

Before deciding anything, locate four things on the page so you are working from your own facts:

  • The tax year or period the balance relates to, and the amount the notice says is owed.
  • The date of the letter and any response date it prints.
  • The address or fax number for hearing requests. The IRS says the hearing address on the notice is different from the address used for payments.
  • The phone number for the office that sent it, in case you want to confirm your account before you decide.

Your options

There are usually three directions, and they are not mutually exclusive. First, you can pay the balance if you are able to. Second, you can ask for a payment arrangement or another collection alternative, and the IRS online payment agreement tool is one place to start. Third, you can request a Collection Due Process hearing with the independent Office of Appeals by completing Form 12153.

A hearing is where Appeals considers collection alternatives. The underlying tax itself can be disputed there only if you did not previously have an opportunity to dispute it, such as an earlier notice you never received. Form 12153 asks you to give a reason for the hearing, so write that reason in plain terms, for example that you want to propose a payment plan because you cannot pay in full today.

A timely request generally pauses levy while the hearing runs. Appeals then issues a determination. After a determination you generally have 30 days to ask the Tax Court to review it. The IRS also describes a faster Collection Appeals Program that cannot be taken to court, so it is worth reading the IRS publication that compares the two before choosing.

What to attach and how to send it

Attach a copy of the notice to Form 12153. If you are proposing a payment plan or other alternative, the IRS says to include the financial statement it names for individuals or businesses. If you are asking for relief as an innocent spouse, that has its own form. If someone else signs for you, include the authorization form that allows it.

Send the package to the hearing address on the notice and keep proof of when you sent it. Consider a method that gives you a receipt. Keep a copy of every page you mail.

If you want a second opinion

The Taxpayer Advocate Service is an independent office inside the IRS that helps people with collection problems, and a tax professional can represent you if you sign the authorization form. Neither is required, and you can handle a hearing request yourself.

Frequently asked questions

Is a CP90 the same as a CP297?

They are the same kind of final notice of intent to levy with Collection Due Process rights. The IRS pages for each give the same basic direction: request a hearing on Form 12153 if you want one, and follow the instructions on your own notice.

Does the IRS take my money the day after the notice?

The notice says the IRS intends to levy, and federal law generally requires written notice of your hearing right at least 30 days before the first levy for that tax period. Your notice controls your actual dates, so read it rather than assuming.

What if I miss the hearing deadline?

The IRS describes a later request as an equivalent hearing, available for a limited time after the levy notice (check the IRS CDP page), but without court review of the result. It is better to send an on-time request if you want the full process.

Can I still arrange a payment plan without a hearing?

Yes. You can contact the IRS about a payment plan or use the online payment agreement tool if you qualify. Requesting a hearing is a separate step that preserves your appeal rights.

Where do I send Form 12153?

To the hearing address printed on your notice, which differs from the payment address. Check the notice itself and the instructions on the form.
