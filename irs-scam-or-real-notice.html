Is This IRS Notice Real? How to Tell Before You Respond

Last updated 2026-11-02 · Guides and rights
Short answer

The IRS usually makes first contact by U.S. mail. Unexpected calls, texts or social media messages that threaten you, rush you, ask for personal information or demand payment deserve suspicion. You can check a real letter through your IRS Online Account or on IRS.gov.

How the IRS usually reaches you

According to the IRS, first contact is usually by U.S. mail. The IRS emails or texts you only if you have opted in to that. A direct message on social media is never from the IRS. It may phone you about an account matter, and unannounced visits are rare, with a letter generally sent first.

That pattern is your best filter. A genuine problem tends to arrive as a letter with a notice number, a response date and instructions. A message that appears out of nowhere on your phone and insists you act in the next few minutes does not fit that pattern.

Warning signs in a message

The IRS says a contact may be a scam if it has any of these traits:

  • It is unexpected, with no earlier letter or contact on the issue.
  • It rushes or threatens you, for example by promising trouble unless you act immediately.
  • It promises a refund, especially one you did not ask about.
  • It asks for personal or financial information, such as account numbers or a login.
  • It demands payment, particularly by gift card or prepaid card. The IRS never accepts gift cards, and neither do its authorized collectors.
  • It arrives as a social media message, which the IRS does not use to start contact.

Check a letter you received in the mail

Mailed letters can be faked too, so a few checks are worthwhile. Find the notice number, which sits in the upper right corner, and search the IRS website for it. A real notice normally has an explanation page there. If the letter does not show up in that search, or still looks suspicious, the IRS notice page says to call the IRS customer service number listed on IRS.gov rather than any number printed only on the suspect letter.

You can also look in your IRS Online Account. It can show balances, payment plan details, and digital copies of some notices and letters, along with transcripts. Not every notice appears there, so a missing letter does not prove a fake. Only you should log in, and always reach the IRS site by typing the address yourself rather than following a link in a message.

Private collection agencies and other callers

Some real collection contacts do not come from the IRS directly. The IRS says private collection agencies it authorizes contact you only after you have received written notice. They also never ask for payment by prepaid card or gift card. Legitimate payment methods are listed on IRS.gov under payments.

If someone calls claiming to be from the IRS or a collector, you can end the call and verify through IRS.gov or your Online Account before you share anything. A real caller will not mind that you checked.

If you think it is a scam

Do not reply, click links or open attachments. For a fake email, the IRS asks you to report it to the phishing address it publishes, using the subject line IRS or Treasury, preferably by forwarding the message as an attachment so identifying details are kept. It also suggests reporting to the Treasury Inspector General for Tax Administration and the Federal Trade Commission, then deleting the email.

The IRS also keeps a current list of scams on its tax scams page. The list changes, so check it if a message looks off. Examples listed include fake charities, impersonators after disasters, and fake offers of Online Account help.

If the notice turns out to be real

Read it carefully and keep it. Note the response date, since replying by the due date is how you keep appeal rights. If you agree, do what the notice asks; if you disagree, follow its instructions and include support. A real notice is not an emergency in itself, but it does set a clock.

Frequently asked questions

Will the IRS call me without sending a letter first?

The IRS says it usually makes first contact by mail, though it may phone about account matters. If a call is unexpected and demands payment or personal information, hang up and check through IRS.gov.

Does the IRS ever message people on social media?

No. The IRS says a social media direct message is never from the IRS and that it does not initiate contact that way.

Can I pay my IRS bill with gift cards?

No. The IRS never accepts gift cards, and neither do its authorized private collectors. Legitimate payment options are on the payments page at IRS.gov.

Where do I report a fake IRS email?

The IRS asks you to send it to its phishing address with the subject IRS or Treasury, preferably forwarded as an attachment. The IRS page on reporting fake messages has the current instructions.

My letter is not in my Online Account. Is it fake?

Not necessarily. The IRS notes that not every notice is available online. Search the notice number on IRS.gov or contact the IRS through the customer service number it publishes.
